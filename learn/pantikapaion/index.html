Pantikapaion: the satyr at the edge of the world

A Greek grain port in the Crimea struck a gold coin that collectors rank beside Kimon's Arethusa, and in 2023 it became the most expensive ancient coin ever sold. Why there, and why so good?

c. 600 BC to the 4th century AD

Pantikapaion at a glance

Pantikapaion, today's Kerch in eastern Crimea, was the capital of the Greek Bosporan kingdom, and for about 75 years it struck some of the finest gold coins of the Greek world.

It sat on the narrow strait between the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov. Behind it lay the steppe of the Scythian horsemen; in front, the sea road to Athens. Its kings grew rich on grain, and from about 380 to 304 BC the city struck gold staters with the same two pictures: a satyr's head and a griffin with a spear in its mouth. One rare issue turned the satyr to look at us, and that coin is the subject of this story. Syracuse made beautiful coins because it was a great power competing with others. Pantikapaion did it at the far edge of the Greek world, which makes it the more surprising.

  • NAC Auction 138 (2023), lots 155–156, catalogue essays
c. 600–575 BC

A Milesian port on the steppe

Greek settlers from Miletus founded Pantikapaion around 600–575 BC, on a hill above the strait the Greeks called the Cimmerian Bosporus.

It became the leading Greek city of the region. To a Greek in Athens it lay at the edge of the known world: beyond it were Scythian nomads, the one-eyed Arimaspians of legend, and the griffins that were said to guard the gold of the north. Around 480 BC the city was ruled by the Archaeanaktid family. In 438/7 BC a man named Spartokos took power, perhaps a Thracian mercenary captain, and founded the Spartokid dynasty, which ruled for more than three hundred years.

The founding date is given as late 7th or early 6th century BC; the city is rarely mentioned by ancient writers, so its early history is thin.

Sources
  • Diodorus of Sicily, Greek historian (1st century BC), 12.31.1: the Archaeanaktids and Spartokos
  • Herodotus, Greek historian of the 5th century BC, 4.13: Arimaspians and griffins
389–349 BC

Grain for Athens

Under King Leukon I, Pantikapaion fed Athens: ships loading grain for Athens went first and paid no export duty.

In a speech of 355/4 BC Demosthenes says about 400,000 medimnoi of grain came to Athens from the Bosporus each year, which he puts at half of what Athens imported. Athens made Leukon a citizen and honoured him with crowns and statues. That wealth paid for the city's gold coinage and for engravers good enough to cut it. The ear of corn under the griffin on every gold stater says where the money came from.

The 400,000 figure comes from a law-court speech and is debated, but most historians take it seriously.

Sources
  • Demosthenes 20 (Against Leptines) 29–40
  • Strabo 7.4.6
  • Moreno, Feeding the Democracy (2007)
c. 380–304 BC

A satyr, a griffin and an ear of corn

Every gold stater of the city shows the same three things: a satyr's head, a horned griffin with a spear in its jaws, and an ear of corn.

The head was long called Pan, a pun on the city's name (Pan-tikapaion), but its features are a satyr's, probably a badge for Satyros I, the king who built the Bosporan kingdom. The griffin is the beast that, Herodotus heard, guarded the gold of Scythia against the Arimaspians: a fitting picture for a gold coin, and much of the gold probably came from Scythian trade. The staters weigh about 9.1 g, a local standard heavier than Athens' 8.6 g, and turn up as far away as a gold hoard on Prinkipo, near Istanbul.

c. 370–350 BC

The satyr turns to face us

For one rare issue the engraver broke the rule and turned the satyr three-quarters towards us: wild hair, a furrowed brow and eyes that seem caught by surprise.

Facing heads were the newest fashion in Greek coins after Kimon's Arethusa at Syracuse around 405 BC, and Larissa, Amphipolis, Ainos, Klazomenai and Rhodes all tried them. They are hard to cut, because the nose and brow point straight out of the die. Pantikapaion's engraver did it best: Godfrey Locker Lampson, who owned a coin from the same dies, called the head 'a marvel of speaking portraiture' and the work of the greatest of all die-engravers. We do not know his name, because no one signed dies here as they did at Syracuse.

MacDonald dates this issue c. 370–355 BC, in Leukon I's reign; NAC and the Royal Library of Belgium give c. 350–300 BC, which could put it under his successors.

c. 370–325 BC

Practising on silver

The small silver coins of the same years show the engravers working out the facing satyr, old and young, with and without ivy, before and after the gold.

On hemidrachms of about 370–355 BC the satyr looks straight out over lions, a lion attacking a stag, and a lion's mask: animal scenes close to the Scythian art the city's goldsmiths knew well. Later drachms bring the three-quarter face back, with a bull's head turning towards us on the other side. The gold stater is the finest form of an idea the workshop kept returning to.

Sources
  • MacDonald 2005, types 40–44 and 57
  • Nomos AG, Auction 39 (2026), lots 114–119 (descriptions)
4th century BC

Why the art is so good here

Money, master goldsmiths, the latest fashion, a workshop that practised, and gold itself. The goldsmiths are the biggest part of the answer.

Money: the grain trade made the Spartokids rich enough to hire the best. Goldsmiths: the same Greek workshops around Pantikapaion made gold for the Scythian kings of the steppe, some of the finest metalwork of the ancient world. The Scythians were nomad horsemen who loved gold and paid for it in grain, horses and slaves, and Greek craftsmen gave them Greek modelling and realism with Scythian subjects and the steppe taste for animals fighting. The Kul-Oba mound, dug up in 1830 just outside the city, held an electrum cup with Scythian warriors bandaging a leg and pulling a tooth, and hundreds of stamped gold plaques sewn onto clothes, many of them faces looking straight out, like the satyr. The Solokha comb shows three warriors fighting over a fallen horse, every strap and wound modelled in the round. The Tovsta Mohyla pectoral, 1.15 kg of gold, has griffins attacking horses: the same beast as on the city's staters. Hands that could chase a lock of hair this finely could cut a die. Fashion: the facing head was the boldest thing a Greek engraver could try after Kimon. Practice: the silver shows the same idea refined over years. Gold: it is soft and does not corrode, so it fills deep dies and keeps every hair. The staters are thick and broad, struck in very high relief with few dies and great care.

None of the Scythian pieces is signed, so which workshop made each one is a scholar's judgement; the Bosporan cities, Pantikapaion first, are the usual answer. That the coin engravers and the goldsmiths were the same people is likely but not recorded. Kul-Oba and Solokha are in the State Hermitage, St Petersburg; the pectoral is in the Museum of Historical Treasures of Ukraine, Kyiv.

c. 355–304 BC

The classic ivy-wreathed stater

After the facing issue the satyr went back to profile, now crowned with ivy, on the stater most museums and collectors know.

They run from Leukon I's last years through Spartokos II and Pairisades I. The best are still superb, struck in high relief on broad flans, and the griffin now carries the letters Π-Α-Ν round it. Gold coinage ended around 304 BC, when the Spartokids took the title of king and, later, put their own portraits on the coins.

Sources
  • MacDonald 2005, types 48, 54 and 63
  • SNG British Museum, Black Sea, 867
304 BC to the 4th century AD

Kings, Mithradates and Caesar

Pantikapaion stayed a royal capital: Mithradates VI of Pontus took it over in 108 BC and died there in 63 BC.

His son Pharnakes II ruled from Pantikapaion and struck gold staters with his own portrait. In 47 BC he fought Julius Caesar at Zela and lost so fast that Caesar wrote 'veni, vidi, vici'. Asandros, who then took the throne, put Nike on a ship's prow on his gold. Bosporan kings went on striking staters under Rome for three more centuries, but the gold slowly turned to electrum and then to base metal. The great age of its art was the 4th century BC.

Sources
  • Appian, Mithridatic Wars 111–120
  • Suetonius, Julius Caesar 37; Plutarch, Caesar 50
  • MacDonald 2005, types 182–216
2012, 2023

A coin worth CHF 5.39 million

In May 2023 a three-quarter satyr stater sold in Zurich for CHF 5.39 million with fees, the highest price ever paid for an ancient coin.

It had already set a record in New York in 2012, at $3.8 million with fees. Very few are known. The Royal Library of Belgium holds three, and in September 2023 Greek police recovered another, better centred, from smugglers in Athens. Valued at about €6 million, it went to the Numismatic Museum of Athens.
